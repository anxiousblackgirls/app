Family and faith can be the strongest support someone with bipolar disorder has. They can also be the source of the most painful misunderstanding. Bringing them into your care, on your terms, makes a measurable difference.

## Family is part of treatment

> In a randomized study, people with bipolar disorder whose families took part in family-focused therapy, which teaches the whole family about the illness, communication, and problem-solving, had fewer relapses and longer stretches of stability than people who got standard care. Families who understand the illness can spot warning signs early and respond with less blame.

## What gets in the way

- Older relatives may call it "nerves," a spiritual attack, or bad behavior
- Some families keep illness secret to protect the family's name
- Some churches teach that enough prayer will heal it, and that medication shows weak faith

A review of faith-based mental health programs with African Americans found that churches can be powerful partners in mental health when they work alongside professional care. Many pastors now encourage members to take their medication and see a therapist.

## Try this

- **Choose one family member to educate first.** Share the NIMH bipolar disorder page or this article.
- **Use a simple script.** "I have bipolar disorder. It's a medical condition. Treatment keeps me well. Here's how you can help."
- **Tell them what to do,** not just what you have. For example: "If I'm up all night two nights in a row, call me and ask if I've talked to my doctor."
- **Talk to your pastor if your church matters to you.** Ask if they know Christian counselors or a mental health ministry. Many will support both prayer and treatment.
- **Ask your provider about family-focused therapy** or family sessions.
