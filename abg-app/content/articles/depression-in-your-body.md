Depression is a whole-body illness. Many Black women first notice it as headaches, back pain, stomach trouble, or exhaustion, not sadness. Doctors don't always connect those symptoms to mood, and some don't take them seriously at all.

## Physical signs of depression

- Ongoing aches and pains with no clear cause
- Headaches or migraines that get worse
- Stomach problems
- Feeling heavy or slowed down
- Sleeping too much or too little
- Changes in appetite

> In a study of white medical students and residents, about half endorsed at least one false belief about biological differences between Black and white people, such as Black skin being thicker. Those who held these beliefs rated a Black patient's pain as lower and gave less accurate treatment recommendations. When Black women describe pain or fatigue, it can be dismissed, and a depression underneath may be missed too.

## Speaking up in the doctor's office

You know your body. Clear, specific information makes it harder for a provider to brush you off, and a depression screen is a quick, standard tool any primary care provider can give.

## Try this

- **Keep a two-week symptom log.** Each day, write your mood from 1 to 10, hours slept, pain level, and energy. Bring it to your appointment.
- **Ask directly.** "Could these symptoms be depression? Can you screen me with the PHQ-9?" The PHQ-9 is a short, widely used depression questionnaire.
- **Bring someone with you.** A friend or family member can take notes and back you up.
- **Ask for it in writing.** If a provider declines a test or referral, you can ask them to note in your chart that you requested it and why it was declined.
- **Get a second opinion** if you feel unheard. You are allowed to change doctors.
