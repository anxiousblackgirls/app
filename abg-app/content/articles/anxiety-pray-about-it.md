Many of us grew up hearing that anxiety was a lack of faith, a phase, or something to keep inside the family. Those messages came from love and from survival. They can also keep you from getting help that works.

## Why we stay quiet

Black adults often report concern about being judged or labeled if they seek mental health care. Some fear being seen as weak. Others have heard stories of doctors who did not listen. Those fears have history behind them, and they are worth naming out loud.

> Faith and therapy can work together. Many Black churches now host counseling ministries, and some therapists bring spirituality into sessions when you ask. You do not have to choose between your God and your peace of mind.

## What help can look like

- **Talking to your doctor.** A primary care visit is a low-pressure place to start. Say "I've been feeling anxious most days and I want to talk about it."
- **Therapy.** A therapist helps you understand your anxiety and practice new ways to respond. You can ask for a Black therapist or one who understands your culture.
- **Medication.** For some people, medication lowers anxiety enough to make daily life and therapy easier. That decision is between you and a prescriber.
- **Community.** Support groups, trusted friends, and faith communities all help. They work best alongside care, not in place of it when anxiety is serious.

## If family pushes back

You can share as much or as little as you want. Try: "I'm taking care of my health. This is part of that." You are allowed to get help before everyone around you understands it.

## Try this

- **Pair a prayer with a step.** Pray about it, then take one action this week: book a doctor's visit, look up a therapist, or tell one person how you're really doing.
- **Ask your church.** Find out if your church has a counseling ministry or a list of Christian counselors.
- **Write your "why."** In one sentence, write why you want help. Read it when doubt or family pushback shows up.
