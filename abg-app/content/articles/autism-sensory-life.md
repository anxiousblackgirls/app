Sounds, lights, textures, and smells can feel much more intense for autistic people. Something other people barely notice, like a humming fridge or a scratchy tag, can take over your whole attention or cause real pain.

## What research shows

> A large review combining many studies found that autistic people are much more likely than non-autistic people to have sensory differences, including being overwhelmed by sensations and sometimes not noticing them enough. These differences show up across all ages.

## Sensory life in Black culture

Some of the most important parts of our lives are also some of the most intense sensory experiences:

- **Hair care.** Hours in the chair, tight braids, scalp pulling, heat, and products with strong smells
- **Church.** Loud music, long services, crowded pews, and hugging
- **Family gatherings.** Many conversations at once, music, cooking smells, and kids running around
- **Being touched.** Relatives who expect hugs and kisses

Many Black autistic women were told they were "tender-headed," "rude," or "acting siddity" when they were actually in sensory overload.

## You can keep what you love

You don't have to give up your culture to protect your senses. You can adjust how you take part.

## Try this

- **Talk to your stylist.** Ask for looser tension, breaks during long appointments, and unscented products. Bring headphones.
- **Choose protective styles that feel good** on your scalp, not just ones that last long.
- **Build a sensory kit:** earplugs or noise-canceling headphones, sunglasses, a fidget, gum, and a soft layer.
- **At gatherings,** find your quiet spot early, like the porch or a back room, and take short breaks before you're overwhelmed.
- **Offer a different greeting.** "I'm not a hugger, but I'm so happy to see you" with a wave or elbow bump is enough.
