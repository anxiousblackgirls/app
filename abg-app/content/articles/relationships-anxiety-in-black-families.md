In many Black families, anxiety doesn't get called anxiety. It gets called "nerves," "pressure," "worrying too much," or "just how Auntie is." It may show up in the body, in control, or in anger long before anyone names it as a mental health condition.

## How it can look different

> Psychologist Angela Neal-Barnett, who studies anxiety in Black women, has described how many Black women talk about their anxiety as "nerves" or stress, and experience it through the body. In her research, she and colleagues tested Sister Circles, small support groups of Black women led in a culturally familiar way, and found they were a promising approach for anxious African American women.

In families, anxiety might look like:

- **Body complaints:** headaches, stomach problems, "my pressure is up"
- **Control:** needing everything done a certain way, or managing everyone's business
- **Overprotection:** not letting kids go anywhere, constant calls to check where you are
- **Anger and irritability:** snapping when stressed, yelling as a way to release worry
- **Constant busyness:** never sitting still because stillness lets the worry in
- **Worst-case thinking:** "Something's going to happen" before every trip

Some of this comes from real danger. Black parents have reason to fear for their children's safety. But fear that never turns off becomes a heavy weight, and children often learn it as their own.

## Seeing it with compassion

Naming anxiety in your family isn't blaming anyone. It can help you understand why your mother couldn't relax, why your father seemed angry, or why you feel on edge too.

## Try this

- **Translate family language.** Next time someone says "my nerves are bad" or "I'm stressed out," gently ask, "Is it more worry, or more tired, or both?"
- **Notice what you inherited.** Write down one worry habit you picked up from family. Ask yourself whether it still keeps you safe or just keeps you scared.
- **Start or join a sister circle.** Gather a few women you trust to talk honestly once a month. Shared stories lower shame.
