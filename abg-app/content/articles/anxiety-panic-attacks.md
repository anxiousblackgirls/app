A panic attack is a sudden wave of intense fear with strong physical symptoms. It peaks within minutes. Many people go to the emergency room the first time because they think something is wrong with their heart.

## What happens in your body

- Pounding or racing heart
- Chest tightness or pain
- Shortness of breath or feeling like you're choking
- Shaking, sweating, or chills
- Dizziness or feeling faint
- Numbness or tingling
- Feeling detached from yourself or your surroundings
- Fear that you're dying or losing control

Panic attacks are frightening, but they are not dangerous on their own. Your body's alarm system has fired at full volume with no real threat present. If you have chest pain and aren't sure what it is, get checked by a doctor.

> In one study, about 59 percent of African Americans with panic disorder reported repeated sleep paralysis, waking up unable to move, often with a sense of a presence in the room. Among white patients with panic disorder the rate was 7 percent. Black participants also reported more early life stress, and the researchers pointed to poverty and racism as likely contributors. In some families this experience is called "the witch riding you." If it happens to you, you are not alone and you are not crazy.

## Getting help

Panic disorder responds well to treatment. Cognitive behavioral therapy teaches you to stop fearing the physical sensations, which takes away much of their power. Medication can also help.

## Try this

When panic starts:

- **Name it.** Say, "This is a panic attack. It will pass."
- **Breathe out longer than you breathe in.** In for four counts, out for six. Long exhales slow your heart rate.
- **Stay where you are if you can.** Leaving teaches your brain the place was dangerous. Let the wave rise and fall.
- **Afterward, write it down.** Note when it happened and what was going on. Patterns help you and your therapist.
