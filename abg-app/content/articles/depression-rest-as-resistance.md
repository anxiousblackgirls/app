Many of us were raised to believe rest is lazy and busy is holy. Our mothers worked double shifts. Our grandmothers never sat down. Rest can feel like something you have to earn, and depression grows in that exhaustion.

## The sleep gap

> A large national study found Black workers were more likely than white workers to get short sleep, under seven hours a night, across most job types. The gap was widest in professional jobs. Poor sleep and depression feed each other: depression disrupts sleep, and too little sleep raises the risk of depression.

Author and activist Tricia Hersey, founder of The Nap Ministry, frames rest for Black people as a form of resistance against a culture that has long valued Black bodies only for their labor. You don't have to agree with every part of that idea to take its main point: your rest has value on its own.

## Rest is more than sleep

Rest can be:

- A 20-minute nap
- Sitting on the porch with no phone
- Saying no to an event so you can stay home
- A slow bath or a long shower
- Daydreaming without trying to be productive
- Prayer, meditation, or quiet worship

## When rest isn't enough

If you rest and still feel exhausted, empty, or hopeless for two weeks or more, that may be depression. Rest supports recovery, but it does not replace treatment.

## Try this

- **Set a wind-down alarm** 45 minutes before your bedtime. When it goes off, screens off and lights low.
- **Keep the same wake time** every day, weekends included. It steadies your body clock more than an early bedtime does.
- **Schedule one rest block this week.** Put it on your calendar like an appointment and protect it.
- **Practice a guilt-free no.** "I'm resting that day" is a complete reason.
