Being the only Black woman, or one of very few, in a workplace or classroom takes energy most people never see. You do the job, and you also manage how you are being seen while you do it.

## What it looks like

- Rehearsing what you'll say in meetings so it lands "right"
- Watching your tone so you don't come across as angry
- Double-checking work so nobody can question your competence
- Feeling drained after work even on an easy day
- Replaying comments at night and wondering if you overreacted

> Researchers interviewed Black women in senior corporate roles about racial microaggressions. The women described colleagues acting surprised by their intelligence, questioning their authority, and leaving them out of meetings and social events. To cope, many leaned on faith and support networks. Many also monitored their speech and felt pressure to never make a mistake, which wore on their confidence over time. A study of Black women college students found similar experiences tied to being both Black and a woman.

That constant monitoring is a form of vigilance. Your body treats every meeting as a possible threat, which is a recipe for anxiety.

## Why it matters

Microaggressions are small moments that add up. Each one alone may seem minor. Together they shape your stress, your sleep, and how safe you feel at work.

## Try this

- **Name it.** When something stings, write one line: what happened, and what it suggested about you. Seeing it on paper helps you separate their bias from your worth.
- **Debrief with someone who gets it.** Pick one person, inside or outside work, you can text after a hard meeting.
- **Set an end to the workday.** Pick a time after which you stop rereading emails and replaying conversations. A short walk or a change of clothes can mark the switch.
- **Keep a wins file.** Save praise, results, and thank-you notes. Read it before reviews and on days you doubt yourself.
