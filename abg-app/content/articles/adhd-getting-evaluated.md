Plenty of women are first diagnosed with ADHD in their 20s, 30s, or 40s, often after a child is diagnosed or life gets too full to keep up. It is never too late to understand your brain.

## Who can evaluate you

Psychiatrists, psychologists, and some primary care doctors and nurse practitioners can diagnose ADHD in adults. A full evaluation usually includes an interview about your life now and as a child, rating questionnaires, and sometimes input from someone who knew you growing up.

## What to bring

- Old report cards. Comments like "doesn't apply herself" or "talks too much in class" are useful evidence.
- A list of everyday struggles: bills paid late, lost keys, unfinished projects, trouble at work.
- Any family history of ADHD, learning differences, anxiety, or depression.
- Notes on how you cope. Overworking and constant list-making are part of the picture.

> Some Black women feel pressure to prove they are "really" struggling because they look successful. Bring examples of what it costs you to keep up, not only what you get done. You are allowed to be capable and still need support.

## About treatment

Treatment can include medication, therapy, ADHD coaching, and changes to your routines and environment. Some people worry about how medication will be seen by family or church. That is a private health decision between you and your prescriber, and you get to decide who knows.

## Try this

Make a one-page summary to bring to your appointment: three struggles from childhood, three from today, and how you cope. Then ask:

- "Do you have experience diagnosing ADHD in adult women?"
- "How do you tell ADHD apart from anxiety or trauma?"
- "What are my options besides medication?"
