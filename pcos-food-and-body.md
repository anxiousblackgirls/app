"Just lose weight" is one of the most common things women with PCOS hear. For many, that advice opens the door to years of dieting, guilt, and a strained relationship with food.

## PCOS and disordered eating

A study at the University of Pennsylvania found that women with PCOS had higher rates of disordered eating than women without it, including binge eating. Hormones and insulin resistance can affect hunger and cravings. Repeated pressure to shrink your body can also lead to cycles of restricting and overeating.

The 2023 international PCOS guideline takes this seriously. It recommends that doctors screen for eating disorders, avoid weight stigma, and recognize that PCOS affects women of every size. It also notes that healthy habits help PCOS symptoms even when the scale doesn't move.

> Black women already carry a lot of messages about our bodies. We're told we're too big, too curvy, or too much, often by people who were never built like us. At the same time, many of us grew up in families where food meant love, celebration, and comfort. When a doctor reduces your PCOS to your weight, it can stir up all of that at once. Eating disorders among Black women are also often missed, because of the myth that they only affect thin white women. If food feels out of control or full of shame, you deserve care for that too.

## Try this

- **Ask your doctor to focus on health markers.** "Can we talk about my blood sugar, blood pressure, and symptoms, and not only my weight?"
- **Be wary of extreme plans.** Programs that cut out whole food groups or promise fast results often backfire. A registered dietitian who understands PCOS and eating disorders can help you build habits that last.
- **Notice how you talk about food.** Labels like "good" and "bad" foods can feed guilt. Try neutral words.
- **Move for how it makes you feel.** Dancing, walking, stretching, and strength work all help insulin resistance and mood.
- **Speak up about eating struggles.** If you binge, restrict, or feel panic around food, tell your doctor or a therapist. You can also contact the National Alliance for Eating Disorders helpline.
