The relationship between a Black mother and daughter can be the deepest love in your life and the source of your hardest lessons. Many of us learned how to be a woman, how to handle pain, and how to survive in this world from our mothers, for better and for worse.

## What mothers pass down

> Research on racial socialization shows that Black parents often teach children about pride in their heritage and prepare them to face discrimination. These lessons protect children. Research on the Strong Black Woman role also shows that many women learned to hide emotions and put others first by watching the women who raised them.

So a mother may have taught you:

- "Don't let them see you cry."
- "You have to be twice as good."
- "What happens in this house stays in this house."
- "Stop being so sensitive."

Those lessons often came from love and fear. A mother who survived hard things wanted to make you strong enough to survive too. Some of those lessons may now be getting in the way of your peace.

## When it gets complicated

Many daughters feel guilt for wanting more emotional closeness, setting boundaries, or going to therapy. Some mothers see a daughter's therapy as criticism of how she was raised. Some daughters are caring for aging mothers while still healing from childhood wounds.

You can hold both truths: your mother did her best with what she had, and you deserve to heal what hurt you.

## Try this

- **Sort the lessons.** Make two lists: lessons from your mother you want to keep, and ones you want to set down. Thank her, even silently, for the first list.
- **Ask about her story.** "What was it like for you growing up?" Many mothers have never been asked. Understanding her pain can soften yours.
- **Use one boundary script.** "I love you, and I'm not going to talk about my weight." Then change the subject.
- **Write the letter you won't send.** Say everything you need to say. Then decide what, if anything, you want to say out loud.
