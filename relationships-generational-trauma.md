Generational trauma, also called intergenerational trauma, is the idea that the effects of trauma can be passed from one generation to the next. It can travel through parenting, family rules, and stress, and possibly through biology.

## What research shows

> A review of research on trauma passed between generations found that children of trauma survivors, including Holocaust survivors, showed differences in stress hormones and higher risk for anxiety and depression. The authors described several possible pathways, including how parents raise children, the stress a mother experiences during pregnancy, and changes in how genes are switched on and off. They noted the biological research is still early.

Dr. Joy DeGruy's theory of Post Traumatic Slave Syndrome describes how the trauma of slavery and ongoing racism may have shaped some patterns in Black families, like strict discipline meant to protect children, or downplaying a child's gifts so they won't become a target. It is a theory, not settled science, but many families recognize their stories in it.

## What it can look like in your family

- Harsh discipline passed down as "this is how we were raised"
- Not talking about pain, illness, or the past
- Fear of authorities, doctors, or outsiders
- Pressure to never show weakness
- Patterns of addiction, violence, or abandonment repeating

## Breaking the cycle

Understanding where a pattern came from doesn't excuse harm, but it helps you choose differently. Every generation that heals makes space for the next one.

## Try this

- **Draw a family map.** Write the names of your parents, grandparents, aunts, and uncles. Next to each, note what you know they lived through and any patterns you see: anxiety, addiction, silence, strength.
- **Pick one pattern to change.** Choose something specific, like "I will talk to my kids about feelings" or "I will apologize when I'm wrong."
- **Collect family stories.** Ask an elder about their life. Record it if they agree. Knowing their strength is part of healing too.
