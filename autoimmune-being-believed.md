"You look fine." "Your labs are normal." "Maybe you're just stressed." If you live with an autoimmune disease, you've probably heard some version of this. Many autoimmune symptoms are invisible, and that can make it hard to get diagnosed, and hard to be believed even after you are.

## Invisible doesn't mean imaginary

Fatigue, joint pain, brain fog and low fevers don't show up in a photo. They can come and go, so you might feel awful on Monday and look fine at your Thursday appointment. Early blood tests can also be unclear. This is part of why many people see several doctors and wait months or years for a diagnosis.

## Bias plays a role

For years, doctors were taught that multiple sclerosis rarely affects Black people, even though newer research shows Black women are at higher risk. And a 2016 study found that many medical trainees believed false ideas about Black people's bodies, like having thicker skin, and those who did rated Black patients' pain as lower.

So if you've felt dismissed, that experience is real and documented. It can also make you dread appointments, cancel follow-ups or stop mentioning symptoms. That reaction makes sense. You still deserve care that takes you seriously.

> In many of our families, going to the doctor was something you did only when you had no choice. Some of us carry stories of relatives who were mistreated, ignored or experimented on. That history is real. You can hold it and still walk into an appointment ready to advocate for yourself, or bring someone who can advocate with you.

## Try this

- **Bring your receipts.** Keep a simple log of symptoms, photos of rashes or swelling, and how symptoms affect your day. See [Bring your receipts](#skill-bring-the-receipts).
- **Lead with impact.** "I've been so tired I've missed work three times this month" is harder to brush off than "I'm tired."
- **Ask directly.** "Could this be autoimmune? What tests would rule it in or out?"
- **Ask for notes in your chart.** If a test or referral is declined, ask that the reason be written down.
- **Bring a second set of ears.** A friend or family member can take notes and speak up.
- **Get a second opinion.** A rheumatologist, neurologist or other specialist may see what a general visit missed.
