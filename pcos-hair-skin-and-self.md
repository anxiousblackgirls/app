Chin hair you pluck every morning. Thinning edges that have nothing to do with braids. Acne that never left after your teens. Dark patches on your neck. These PCOS symptoms show up where people can see them, and they can change how you feel walking into a room.

## Why these symptoms hit hard

PCOS raises androgens, hormones like testosterone. Higher androgens can cause hair growth on the face, chest, or stomach, hair loss on the scalp, and acne. Insulin resistance can cause acanthosis nigricans, darker, velvety skin in body folds. The 2023 international PCOS guideline lists these as core symptoms that deserve treatment, and it recommends that doctors ask how they affect your quality of life.

Research links PCOS with much higher rates of anxiety and depression, and visible symptoms are part of that weight.

> For many Black women, hair is personal. It's tied to family, culture, and how we've been judged at school and work. Losing hair can feel like losing part of your identity, and it often gets blamed on your styling choices before anyone checks your hormones. Facial hair can bring a deep shame that many of us never talk about, even with friends. The 2023 guideline also notes that the hirsutism scale doctors use was built mostly on white women, so your provider should consider your ethnicity when judging what's typical for you.

## Try this

- **Tell your doctor exactly what's bothering you.** "The hair on my chin affects my confidence every day. What treatments are available?" Medications can lower androgen effects.
- **Ask about hair loss specifically.** Mention when it started and where. Ask whether a dermatologist who treats Black hair and scalps can take a look.
- **Choose hair removal that's safe for your skin.** Laser and other treatments work differently on darker skin, so ask whether the provider has experience with skin like yours.
- **Notice the stories you tell yourself.** Thoughts like "I'm less of a woman" deserve a second look. Write them down and ask whether you'd say them to a friend.
- **Talk about it.** Shame grows in silence. A therapist or a PCOS support group can be a place to say the things you've kept to yourself.
