Grief usually softens with time. The waves come less often and hurt a little less. For some people, though, grief stays so intense that daily life stops working. That has a name now, and it can be treated.

## Prolonged grief disorder

> In 2022, the American Psychiatric Association added prolonged grief disorder to its diagnostic manual (DSM-5-TR). It describes grief that lasts at least a year after a death for adults, with intense longing for or constant thoughts about the person most days, plus symptoms like feeling that part of yourself died, disbelief, avoiding reminders, intense emotional pain, trouble re-engaging with life, numbness, or feeling life is meaningless.

This isn't about how much you loved them or how long is "too long" to miss someone. It's about grief that keeps you from living.

## Through a Black lens

Many Black women don't get to grieve fully. We plan the funeral, handle the paperwork, take care of everyone else, and go back to work. The grief we postponed can come back months or years later, sometimes as depression, anxiety, or physical illness. Repeated losses can also pile on top of each other before we've healed from the last one.

## Getting help

Grief therapy works, including a specific approach called prolonged grief disorder therapy. A therapist can also help untangle grief from depression, which can happen at the same time.

## Try this

Answer these honestly:

- Has it been more than a year, and do I still think about them most of the day?
- Do I avoid places, people, or things because they remind me of the loss?
- Does life feel empty or pointless without them?
- Am I struggling to work, care for myself, or connect with others?

If you answered yes to several, reach out to a therapist and say, "I lost someone and I'm not able to function the way I used to." Ask if they have experience with grief.

If you are having thoughts of ending your life to be with the person you lost, call 911 or call/text 988 right now.
