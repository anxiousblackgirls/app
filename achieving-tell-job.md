You just got a diagnosis, or you've had one for years, and work is getting harder. Should you tell your boss? HR? Nobody at all? There's no single right answer. It depends on what you need, how safe your workplace feels and what you want to protect.

## Why some women tell

- To ask for changes that make work easier, like flexible hours, a quiet space or written instructions
- So time off for appointments or flares isn't held against them
- Because hiding it takes a lot of energy

## Why some women don't

- Worry about being seen as less capable, or passed over for promotion
- A workplace that already feels unsafe
- Not needing any changes right now

For Black women, the worry is often heavier. Many of us already feel like we have to work twice as hard to be seen as competent, so sharing a health condition can feel risky. Those concerns are valid.

## What the law says (U.S.)

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) covers many health conditions, including mental health conditions, ADHD, autism and autoimmune diseases, when they significantly affect daily life. If your employer has 15 or more employees, they generally must provide reasonable accommodations unless it would cause them significant hardship. You usually need to tell your employer about your condition to ask for one, and they may ask for a note from your provider. You don't have to share your full medical history, and your employer must keep your medical information confidential. The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) may also protect up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave a year if you qualify.

The Job Accommodation Network (askjan.org) is a free service that can help you figure out what to ask for.

> Many of us learned to keep our business private at work and never let them see you struggle. That habit protected a lot of women. You still get to choose. Asking for what you need isn't weakness. It's how you keep doing work you're proud of.

## Try this

- **Decide what you need first.** Write down two or three changes that would help the most.
- **Start with the least you need to share.** "I have a health condition, and these changes would help me do my best work."
- **Put it in writing.** Follow up any conversation with an email.
- **Keep records.** Save emails, notes and dates.
- **Know your leave options.** See [Use the leave you've earned](#article-achieving-use-your-leave).
- **Get help if things go wrong.** If you're treated unfairly, you can contact the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (eeoc.gov).
