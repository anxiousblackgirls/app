Getting fertility care as a Black woman can mean fighting for referrals, paying out of pocket, and sitting in waiting rooms where no one looks like you. It can also mean having concerns brushed off until precious time has passed.

## Where the gaps are

Black women face barriers at nearly every step:

- **Getting in the door.** Black women are less likely than white women to seek fertility treatment and often wait longer to do so. Cost is a big reason, and use of fertility treatment rises when insurance covers it.
- **Starting treatment.** A study at a large New York clinic found that Black patients took longer to begin treatment after their first visit than white patients.
- **Feeling understood.** In one survey, nearly half of Black women said their doctor didn't understand their cultural background.
- **Outcomes.** A review of IVF studies found that Black women generally had lower pregnancy and live birth rates than white women.

Fibroids play a part. One study found that by age 50, more than 80 percent of Black women had developed fibroids, compared with about 70 percent of white women. Fibroids can affect fertility, so they deserve attention early.

> Many Black women have been told to "just relax" or "keep trying" for years before anyone runs a test. In one study of Black women with infertility, about a quarter felt that racism, sexism, or class bias affected their care, including doctors making assumptions about their sex lives and finances. Being dismissed during something this personal can leave you anxious, angry, and doubting yourself. You're allowed to ask questions, push for answers, and switch doctors.

## Try this

- **Know when to ask for help.** Doctors generally recommend a fertility evaluation after a year of trying if you're under 35, or after six months if you're 35 or older. Ask sooner if you have irregular periods, fibroids, or PCOS.
- **Ask about fibroids directly.** "Could fibroids be affecting my fertility? Can we check?"
- **Check your insurance and state laws.** Some states require insurance to cover fertility care.
- **Interview clinics.** Ask about their experience treating Black patients and what support they offer.
- **Bring a support person** to take notes and help you ask questions.
- **Take care of your mind during the process.** A therapist can help you handle the stress of appointments, results, and waiting.
