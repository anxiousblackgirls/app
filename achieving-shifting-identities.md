At work you speak one way, wear your hair one way, laugh at certain jokes and not others. At home or with your girls, you're someone else entirely. Many Black women live in two worlds every day, and switching between them takes energy.

## Shifting at work

> Researchers interviewed early-career Black women about how they change their identities at work. Women described shifting their speech, appearance, and behavior to fit in and avoid stereotypes, such as being seen as angry or unprofessional. Shifting helped some advance their careers. It also brought costs, including feeling inauthentic, emotional exhaustion, and losing touch with who they are. Research on code-switching among Black professionals has found similar tradeoffs.

## What shifting can cost

- Feeling drained after work even on a light day
- Never feeling fully known by coworkers
- Second-guessing your natural voice, style, or opinions
- Feeling like a different person at home and at work
- Resentment that others don't have to work this hard to belong

## Through a Black lens

Our grandmothers and mothers shifted too, often to survive. It's a skill, and sometimes a necessary one. You get to decide when shifting serves you and when it costs more than it gives. More states have passed laws like the CROWN Act, which bans hair discrimination at work and school, so some of the pressure to change your appearance has legal pushback now.

## Try this

- **Track your shifts** for one day: when you changed your voice, hair, topic, or reaction to fit in. Note how you felt after each.
- **Pick one place to stop shifting.** Maybe it's wearing your natural hair to one meeting, or saying what you actually think in one conversation.
- **Find your unfiltered spaces.** Friends, groups, or communities where you never have to translate yourself.
- **Decompress on purpose.** Give yourself 10 minutes after work to come back to yourself before you walk in the door at home.
