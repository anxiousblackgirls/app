College, grad school, law school, med school. For many Black women, higher education carries the hopes of a whole family. It can also bring isolation, impostor feelings, and pressure that nobody else in the family has faced.

## What research shows

> A study of Black women college students found they experienced gendered racial microaggressions on campus, like being stereotyped as angry or overlooked in class, and coped through community with other Black women, self-protection, and pushing back. Research with ethnic minority students, including African American students, found that impostor feelings were strongly linked to depression and anxiety, more strongly than the general stress of being a minority on campus.

## Pressures you might face

- Being the first in your family to go to college or grad school
- Being one of few Black women in your program
- Sending money home or helping family while in school
- Student loan debt that feels overwhelming
- Professors or classmates who question whether you belong
- Feeling like you can't fail because so many people are counting on you

## Through a Black lens

Even surrounded by classmates, many of us feel alone on campus. The pressure to be twice as good, and to make everyone proud, can turn into burnout and anxiety.

## Try this

- **Find your people.** Join a Black student union, a sorority, a Black graduate student association, or a group for students in your field.
- **Use campus counseling early,** before a crisis. Ask if they have Black therapists or someone who understands first-generation students.
- **Set one limit with family.** Explain what you can and can't do while you're in school, especially around money and time.
- **Talk to a mentor.** A Black woman faculty member or professional in your field can help you see the path ahead.
- **Remember why you started.** Write it down and keep it where you'll see it.
