Infertility is often pictured as a white woman's struggle, and that picture leaves Black women out. Many Black women go through it quietly, without seeing anyone who looks like them going through the same thing.

## It's more common than people think

A study of Black and white women ages 33 to 44 found that Black women had about twice the odds of infertility compared with white women, even after accounting for income, smoking, weight, and fibroids. Yet research also shows Black women are less likely to seek fertility treatment and often wait longer before they do.

> Researchers at the University of Michigan interviewed 50 Black women who had experienced infertility. Nearly all of them coped in silence, even when friends or family knew. Many said they'd absorbed the idea that a woman isn't complete without a child. One said not having biological children "would label you as a failure." Some felt shame shaped by religious beliefs about a woman's purpose, and about a quarter felt that racism, sexism, or class bias affected how doctors treated them. The researchers linked the silence to expectations that Black women stay strong and private, and to how rarely Black women with infertility are seen in public.

## What silence costs

Keeping infertility to yourself can protect you from hurtful comments. It can also leave you carrying grief, anger, and fear with no place to put them. Over time, that isolation can feed anxiety and depression.

## Try this

- **Choose one safe person to tell.** You don't have to tell everyone. One friend, sister, or partner who listens without fixing can make a difference.
- **Find stories like yours.** Groups and podcasts led by Black women going through infertility can help you feel less alone.
- **Prepare for hard questions.** "We're not sharing family plans right now" is a full answer to "When are you having kids?"
- **Notice self-blame.** Infertility is a medical condition. It isn't punishment, and it doesn't measure your worth as a woman.
- **Get support for your mental health.** A therapist who understands infertility can help you cope with the waiting, the treatments, and the grief.
