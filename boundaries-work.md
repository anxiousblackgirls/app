You're the one who plans the office party, takes notes in every meeting, mentors every new Black hire, and sits on the diversity committee. None of it is in your job description. None of it gets you promoted.

## What research shows

> Economists found that women are asked to take on "non-promotable" tasks, like planning events and serving on committees, more often than men, and are more likely to say yes when asked. These tasks take time away from work that leads to raises and promotion. The researchers later wrote "The No Club," a guide to spreading this work more fairly. Lean In and McKinsey's Women in the Workplace research has found that Black women are often expected to do extra work while getting less support from managers.

## Signs you need a work boundary

- Your calendar is full of things that aren't your actual job
- You answer emails at night and on weekends
- You're the go-to person for every "people" problem
- You feel you can't say no without hurting your reputation
- You're exhausted but not advancing

## Through a Black lens

Saying no at work can feel risky when you're one of few Black women, or when your job feels insecure. Many of us also want to lift up others who look like us, which is meaningful. Boundaries help you choose that work on purpose instead of having it pile up.

## Try this

- **Sort your tasks.** List your work for the week and mark each one "promotable" (it's in your job description or gets noticed by leaders) or "non-promotable." Aim to reduce the second list.
- **Use a redirect:** "I've done the last three. Could someone else take this one?"
- **Ask for credit:** "I'm glad to lead the committee. Can we include it in my performance goals?"
- **Set a sign-off time** and turn off work notifications after it.
- **Keep your yes for what matters most** to you, like mentoring one person well instead of five people thinly.
