Your doctor says your pressure is high, again. Maybe your mother, aunties and grandmother all had "pressure" too, so it almost feels expected. But high blood pressure isn't just something that runs in the family. For many Black women, it's also a sign of how much stress our bodies have been carrying.

## How common it is

About 58 percent of Black women in the United States have high blood pressure, one of the highest rates in the world, according to the American Heart Association. Many of us develop it younger, sometimes in our 20s and 30s, and it raises the risk of stroke, heart disease, kidney disease and problems during pregnancy. Because it often has no symptoms, many women don't know they have it.

## Stress and racism raise the pressure

Genes and salt are only part of the story. In the Jackson Heart Study, which followed Black adults in Mississippi, people who reported more discrimination over their lifetime were more likely to develop high blood pressure, even after researchers accounted for age, income, weight, smoking and other risk factors. When your body is on guard day after day, stress hormones keep your heart rate and blood pressure up. Over years, that wear and tear adds up.

## What your pressure might be telling you

High blood pressure can be a signal to look at your whole life, not just your salt shaker:

- Are you sleeping enough, or staying up because it's the only quiet time you get?
- Are you caring for everyone else and skipping your own appointments?
- Are you dealing with stress at work, money worries or a relationship that drains you?
- Are you holding feelings in because you have to keep it together?

> In many of our families, "pressure" was talked about like the weather. Grandma had it, Mom has it, so we'll get it too. But our elders often carried heavy loads with very little support. Knowing that can help you choose differently: getting checked early, taking your medicine and making room for rest.

## Try this

- **Know your numbers.** Get your blood pressure checked, or use a home monitor. Write down your readings.
- **Take medicine as prescribed.** If it's too expensive or the side effects bother you, tell your doctor. There are other options.
- **Slow your breathing.** A few minutes of slow breathing a day can help your body calm down. Try [Box breathing](#skill-box-breathing).
- **Notice your stress signals.** Try [What's my body telling me?](#skill-body-signal-check).
- **Look at the bigger picture.** Read [When your body sounds the alarm](#article-health-body-alarm).
