Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a hormone condition that affects how your ovaries work, how your body uses insulin, and sometimes how you feel about yourself. The World Health Organization estimates it affects 6 to 13 percent of women of reproductive age, and up to 70 percent of them don't know they have it.

## What it looks like

Doctors usually diagnose PCOS when you have two of these three signs:

- **Irregular or missing periods**, or cycles where you don't ovulate.
- **Signs of higher androgens** (hormones like testosterone), such as acne, extra hair on the face or body, or thinning hair on your scalp.
- **Many small follicles on your ovaries** seen on ultrasound.

PCOS can also bring insulin resistance, weight changes, skin darkening on the neck or underarms, trouble getting pregnant, and mood changes. The 2023 international guideline on PCOS calls it a lifelong condition that touches your heart health, fertility, and mental health, so it deserves more than a quick birth control prescription.

> PCOS can look different in Black women, and much of the research was done on white women. One large U.S. study found Black women with PCOS had lower triglycerides and lower rates of metabolic syndrome than Hispanic women, even at similar body sizes. That kind of difference can lead a doctor to say your labs look "fine" when your symptoms say otherwise. Many of us also grew up being told irregular periods, acne, or chin hair were just "how our bodies are," so we stopped asking questions.

## Why it affects your mind too

Living with symptoms that change your skin, hair, weight, and cycle can wear on your confidence. Women with PCOS report much higher rates of anxiety and depression than women without it. If you've felt down, on edge, or ashamed and didn't know why, PCOS may be part of the picture.

## Try this

- **Track your cycle** for three months, including spotting, skipped periods, and symptoms like acne or hair growth. A record is harder to dismiss than a memory.
- **Ask directly:** "Could these symptoms be PCOS? What tests would rule it in or out?"
- **Ask about insulin and heart health,** not only your period.
- **Check in with your mood.** If PCOS is weighing on you, tell your doctor and consider a therapist who understands chronic health conditions.
