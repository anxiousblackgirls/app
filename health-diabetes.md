You got the call: your A1C is high. Maybe it's prediabetes, maybe it's type 2 diabetes. Maybe you saw it coming, or maybe it hit like a ton of bricks. Either way, a diabetes diagnosis affects more than your blood sugar. It affects your mood, your relationship with food and how you feel about yourself.

## You're not alone

About 12 percent of Black adults in the United States have been diagnosed with diabetes, compared with about 7 percent of white adults, according to the CDC. Many more have prediabetes and don't know it. Diabetes is linked to genes, but also to stress, sleep, access to healthy food and safe places to move, and the cost of care.

## Stress and blood sugar are connected

When you're stressed, your body releases hormones like cortisol that raise your blood sugar so you have energy to handle a threat. That's helpful in an emergency. But when stress never lets up, your blood sugar can stay high. Poor sleep and emotional eating can push it higher too.

## Diabetes and your mood

People with diabetes are about twice as likely to have depression as people without it, according to a review of 42 studies. Many also feel "diabetes distress": the worry, frustration and burnout of checking numbers, watching every bite and feeling judged. When you're down, it's harder to take care of your diabetes, and when your diabetes is hard to manage, it's easier to feel down. Breaking that cycle often means caring for both.

> Food is love in our families: Sunday dinner, rice and peas, mac and cheese, jollof, sweet potato pie, Grandma's pound cake. A diabetes diagnosis can make it feel like you have to give up your culture. You don't. Many of our dishes can be adjusted, and you can still sit at the table. Being there matters as much as what's on your plate.

## Try this

- **Ask about diabetes education.** Many clinics and insurance plans cover classes with a nurse or dietitian. Ask for one who understands the foods you eat.
- **Watch for signs of depression or burnout.** If you've stopped checking your numbers or feel hopeless, tell your doctor or a therapist.
- **Make small swaps, not big rules.** Try brown rice some nights, a smaller slice of cake or a walk after dinner.
- **Check your stress.** Try [What's my body telling me?](#skill-body-signal-check) when your numbers are off and you don't know why.
- **Get support.** A friend, a support group or a church health ministry can make it feel less lonely.
