Living with an autoimmune disease is hard on your body. It's also hard on your mind. If you've felt anxious, low, short-tempered or not like yourself since your symptoms started, you're not imagining it, and you're not weak.

## Depression and anxiety are common

Researchers who pooled dozens of studies found that about one in four people with lupus had major depression, and anxiety was even more common. Other autoimmune diseases show similar patterns. Some of it comes from living with pain, fatigue and uncertainty. Some of it may come from the disease itself, because inflammation can affect the brain and mood.

## Stress and trauma matter too

The connection goes both ways. A study of more than 100,000 people in Sweden found that people diagnosed with stress-related conditions, like PTSD, were more likely to develop an autoimmune disease later. In a long-term study of U.S. women, those who had been through trauma were more likely to develop lupus.

Racism is a stressor too. In the BeWELL study, researchers followed hundreds of Black women with lupus in Atlanta. Women who reported more racial discrimination had more lupus activity and more organ damage. Even hearing about or watching racism happen to others was linked to more disease activity.

None of this means you caused your illness. It means your mind, your body and the world you live in are connected, and caring for your mental health is part of caring for your disease.

> We're often told to "be strong" and "don't claim it." But naming what's happening in your body and mind isn't giving up. You can have faith, keep your strength and still say, "This is hard, and I need support."

## Signs to talk to someone

- Feeling down, numb or hopeless most days for two weeks or more
- Losing interest in things you used to enjoy
- Constant worry about your health, flares or the future
- Pulling away from people because explaining feels like too much
- Thoughts that everyone would be better off without you

If you're having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988.

## Try this

- **Tell your rheumatologist how you're feeling.** Mood changes can be part of the disease or a side effect of some medicines, like steroids.
- **Find a therapist who understands chronic illness.** Therapy can help you cope with flares, grief and uncertainty.
- **Limit the news when you need to.** Taking breaks from stories about racism is not ignoring the world. It's protecting your health.
- **Try a calming skill daily.** Start with [The physiological sigh](#skill-physiological-sigh) or [Pace, don't push](#skill-pacing).
