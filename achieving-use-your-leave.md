You've worked through migraines, panic attacks, a parent's surgery and a loss that still hurts. You've saved your sick days "for something serious." Many Black women treat time off like a luxury we haven't earned. But leave protections exist for exactly the moments we keep pushing through, and using them is one of the ways we protect our health.

## Many of us need leave and don't take it

In a 2018 U.S. Department of Labor survey, 11 percent of Black workers said they needed time off for a family or medical reason in the past year and didn't take it, compared with 6 percent of white workers. The most common reason was simple: they couldn't afford unpaid leave. An analysis by the Center for American Progress and the National Partnership for Women & Families estimated that working Black women need about 2.9 million leaves each year, and about 1.1 million of them never happen.

Some of this is about money. Many Black women are the main earner in their household, and unpaid time off can feel impossible. Some of it is about fear: of looking weak, of being labeled unreliable or of being first on the layoff list. Those worries come from real experiences.

## What pushing through costs

Researchers describe a "Superwoman schema" that many Black women carry: the pressure to look strong, hide struggle, help everyone and never ask for help. Women in Cheryl Woods-Giscombé's research described how it helped them survive, and how it also left them exhausted, stressed and putting their own health last. Working while sick or burned out can make depression, anxiety and physical conditions worse. A study of U.S. workers found that people without paid sick leave reported more psychological distress than people who had it.

## Tools you may already have

- **FMLA.** The Family and Medical Leave Act can give you up to 12 weeks of job-protected leave a year for your own serious health condition, including mental health conditions like depression, anxiety or PTSD that need ongoing treatment. You can take it all at once or in pieces, like a few hours a week for therapy. It's usually unpaid, and you generally qualify if your employer has 50 or more employees nearby and you've worked there at least a year (about 1,250 hours).
- **Short-term disability.** Many employers offer insurance that pays part of your salary when you can't work because of an illness, including mental health conditions. A few states, including California, New York, New Jersey, Rhode Island and Hawaii, also have state disability programs.
- **State paid leave.** More than a dozen states and Washington, D.C., now have paid family and medical leave programs. Check if yours does.
- **Your Employee Assistance Program (EAP).** Many jobs offer a few free, confidential counseling sessions. Look in your benefits portal.
- **ADA accommodations.** You can ask for changes like a flexible schedule or remote days. See [Should I tell my job about my diagnosis?](#article-achieving-tell-job).
- **Your paid time off.** It's part of your pay. Using it for your mental health is a good use of it.

> Our grandmothers worked through everything because they had no protections at all. Many of these laws exist because people fought for them. Using them isn't weakness or ingratitude. It's using what was won for us.

## Try this

- **Read your benefits.** Find your handbook or benefits portal and look up FMLA, short-term disability, EAP and paid time off.
- **Talk to your doctor or therapist early.** They can fill out FMLA or disability forms and help you decide how much time you need.
- **Ask HR in writing.** "I'd like information about FMLA and short-term disability options for a health condition."
- **Plan for money.** Ask whether you can use paid time off during FMLA, and when disability pay starts.
- **Know where to get help.** The U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division (dol.gov/whd) answers FMLA questions.
