Borderline personality disorder rarely comes from nowhere. For many people, it grows out of a childhood where emotions weren't safe, needs weren't met, or harm happened.

## What research shows

> A study of people with BPD found that most reported painful childhood experiences, including abuse, neglect, and caregivers who were emotionally withdrawn or inconsistent. These experiences were much more common than among people with other personality disorders. Researchers today see BPD as a mix of an emotionally sensitive temperament and an environment that didn't help a child learn to handle big feelings.

That doesn't mean your family is to blame for everything, or that everyone with hard childhoods develops BPD. It means your reactions make sense given what you lived through.

## Through a Black lens

Many Black girls grow up being treated as older than they are. Research on adultification shows adults see Black girls as needing less comfort and protection than white girls the same age. Some of us were told to stop crying, handle it, and be strong long before we were able to. Add family stress, poverty, or racism, and a sensitive child may never get the comfort that teaches her to calm down.

## Healing is possible

Therapies like dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) and trauma-focused therapy help people build the skills they didn't get to learn as children. Most people with BPD improve a great deal over time.

## Try this

- **Validate yourself.** When a strong emotion hits, finish this sentence: "It makes sense I feel ___ because ___." Validation was missing for many of us growing up. You can start giving it to yourself.
- **Find your younger self.** Look at a childhood photo. Write one thing that girl needed to hear. Read it back to yourself on hard days.
- **Ask about trauma-informed therapy** when you look for a therapist. You can say, "I think my childhood affects how I handle emotions. Do you work with that?"
