In many Black communities, a funeral is a homegoing: a celebration of a life and a return home. Our traditions around death carry deep wisdom. They can also leave little room for the grief that comes after the repast is over.

## Our traditions

> Scholar Karla FC Holloway's history of African American mourning describes how Black communities built their own rituals around death: the homegoing service, the gathering at the house, the food, the music, the obituary program kept for years. These traditions grew partly because Black families faced so much early and violent death, and needed ways to honor their dead with dignity.

These rituals hold us together. Family shows up. Church members bring food. Everyone sings.

## What can get lost

- After the funeral, everyone goes home and the calls stop
- "They're in a better place" can feel like you're not allowed to be sad
- Strong women are expected to plan everything and not break down
- Anger at God, at doctors, or at the person who died can feel forbidden

## Making space for your grief

You can honor your faith and your traditions and still give your grief room. Grief and faith can live together.

## Try this

- **Ask for help after the funeral, not just before.** Tell two people, "Can you check on me every week for the next two months?"
- **Create a small ritual of your own.** Light a candle on their birthday, cook their favorite dish, play their song, or visit their resting place.
- **Keep the obituary program somewhere you can see it,** or start a memory box with photos and small items.
- **Let yourself feel all of it.** Write a letter to the person who died, including the parts that are angry or unfinished.
- **Talk with your pastor or a grief counselor** if your faith feels shaken. Doubt is a common part of grief.
