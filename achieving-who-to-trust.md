Your manager says her door is always open. HR says they're here for you. The coworker who sits next to you seems sweet. So why does your gut say to be careful? For many Black women, knowing who's safe at work is a skill learned the hard way.

## Support isn't spread evenly

Research shows Black women often get less backing at work. In a 2019 study of Black professionals, only 19 percent of Black women said people in their network advocated for their ideas and skills, compared with 35 percent of white women. Lean In found that fewer than a quarter of Black women felt they had the sponsorship they needed to move up, and that they were less likely than white women to have managers who showcased their work.

## Mentors, sponsors and friends are different

- **A mentor** gives advice and helps you think things through.
- **A sponsor** has power and uses it for you. She says your name in rooms you're not in and puts you up for stretch projects.
- **A work friend** makes the day easier, but may not be the right person for sensitive information.
- **HR** exists to protect the company. They can be helpful, and their job is to manage risk, so go in prepared.

## Signs someone is safe

- They keep small confidences and don't share other people's business with you.
- They credit your ideas in meetings, especially when you're not there.
- Their words in private match their actions in public.
- They don't ask you to be the spokesperson for all Black people.

> Our communities taught us to be careful about who knows our business: "Don't tell everybody everything." At work, that instinct can protect you. It can also keep you isolated. Being guarded with some people and open with a trusted few can both be true. Many Black women find their safest people in other Black women at work, employee resource groups, sorority sisters or professional networks outside the company.

## Try this

- **Share in layers.** Give a little and see what someone does with it before sharing more.
- **Look for a sponsor, not just a mentor.** Ask, "Who has influence over my next role, and do they know my work?"
- **Go to HR with notes.** Bring dates, facts and what you're asking for. Follow up in writing.
- **Build a circle outside your company.** A trusted group of peers can give you a straight read on what's happening.
- **Trust your gut, then check it.** Notice the feeling, then look at the person's actions over time.
