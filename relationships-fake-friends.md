You leave the brunch feeling worse than when you got there. She only calls when she needs something. Your good news gets a lukewarm "congrats." Some friendships quietly drain you, and for anxious women, they can keep your body on edge long after you've left the room.

## Mixed friendships take a toll

Research on social connection is clear: strong relationships protect your health. A review of 148 studies found people with stronger social relationships had a 50 percent greater chance of survival over time. But not every relationship helps. One study found that interacting with people you feel mixed about, sometimes supportive and sometimes hurtful, raised blood pressure more than time with people who were consistently supportive. The friend you can never quite relax around may be costing you more than you realize.

## Signs a friendship isn't safe

- You edit yourself around her, or feel anxious before you see her.
- Your wins get competition, silence or a quick change of subject.
- She shares other people's business, and you wonder what she says about yours.
- The friendship only works when you do the reaching out, listening and fixing.
- You feel relief when plans get canceled.

> Black women are often taught to keep friendships at all costs: loyalty, sisterhood, "we don't leave each other." That loyalty is beautiful when it goes both ways. It turns harmful when it means staying close to someone who doesn't treat you well. At the same time, the right friends can be healing. Psychologist Angela Neal-Barnett developed Sister Circles, small support groups for anxious Black women, after professional Black women in her research described sisterhood as one of the most trusted ways they cope with anxiety. Good friendships can work like that every day.

## Vetting new friends, gently

You don't need a background check, just patience. Watch how someone treats people who can't do anything for them. Notice whether they keep small promises, keep your secrets and celebrate your news. Trust builds through time and consistency, not intensity. A new friend who wants to be your best friend in two weeks is worth slowing down with.

## Try this

- **Do a gut check.** After you spend time with someone, ask: Do I feel lighter or heavier?
- **Create distance without drama.** You can answer less often, decline some invites and keep conversations surface-level.
- **Have the honest conversation** when the friendship matters: "I've noticed I'm usually the one reaching out. Is everything okay with us?"
- **Share in layers.** Give new friends small pieces of yourself and see what they do with them.
- **Notice your anxiety.** If you worry constantly that friends are upset with you, that may be anxiety talking. A therapist can help you tell the difference.
