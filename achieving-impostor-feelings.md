You got the degree, the promotion, the title. Part of you still waits for someone to find out you don't belong. Many high-achieving Black women know that feeling well.

## Impostor feelings

Psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes first described the impostor phenomenon in 1978: high-achieving people who believe their success is luck or a mistake, and fear being exposed as frauds.

> A review of 62 studies found impostor feelings are common and linked to anxiety, depression, and burnout. Research with African American, Asian American, and Latino college students found that impostor feelings were a stronger predictor of depression and anxiety than the stress of being a minority on campus.

## Why it hits Black women

Impostor feelings aren't only in your head. When you're the only Black woman in the room, when people question your credentials, or mistake you for the assistant, the environment is telling you that you don't belong. "Twice as good" was a survival lesson, and it can turn into feeling you're never good enough.

## How it shows up

- Overpreparing for everything
- Downplaying your accomplishments
- Fear of asking questions in case you look unqualified
- Not applying for roles unless you meet every requirement
- Feeling anxious after praise instead of proud

## Try this

- **Keep an evidence file.** Save emails with praise, results you delivered, and thank-you notes. Read it before big meetings and reviews.
- **Rewrite the thought.** Swap "I got lucky" for "I prepared, I worked, and I earned this."
- **Name the room, not just yourself.** Ask: "Is this feeling about me, or about a place that wasn't built with me in mind?"
- **Find your circle.** Talk with other Black women in your field. You'll likely find they feel it too, and seeing them clearly helps you see yourself clearly.
