You raise a concern in a meeting and someone says, "I'm sure that's not what he meant." Your idea gets ignored, then praised when a coworker repeats it. You're told you're "too sensitive" or "reading into things." After a while, you start wondering if the problem is you.

## What racial gaslighting looks like

Gaslighting is when someone makes you doubt what you saw, heard or felt. Political scientists Angelique Davis and Rose Ernst describe "racial gaslighting" as the way people and institutions deny or explain away racism, so the person who names it ends up looking like the problem. At work it can sound like:

- "You're overthinking it." "That's just how she talks."
- "We don't see color here."
- Vague feedback, like "polish" or "executive presence," with no clear examples
- Being told an incident "wasn't a big deal" after you report it

## It isn't in your head

In a 2020 report on Black women in corporate America, Lean In found that more than 1 in 4 Black women had heard coworkers express surprise at their language skills or other abilities, compared with about 1 in 7 women overall. Black women were also more likely to say they had to prove their competence again and again. When the slights are small and constant, it's easy for others to dismiss each one, and easy for you to doubt yourself.

> Many of us grew up hearing "don't make a fuss" or "be grateful you have a job," especially in immigrant households from the Caribbean, Africa and Latin America. Keeping quiet can feel like protection. But when you can't trust your own read on a situation, your anxiety gets louder. Your perceptions are data, and you're allowed to take them seriously.

## Try this

- **Write it down the same day.** Date, time, who was there, what was said, how you responded. Facts only. A record helps you trust yourself, and it helps if you ever need to report something.
- **Ask for specifics.** "Can you give me an example of what 'more polish' would look like?" Vague feedback is hard to act on and easy to use against you.
- **Reality-check with someone you trust.** A friend in your field, a mentor outside your company or a therapist can tell you, "No, that wasn't okay."
- **Restate your point.** "I want to make sure my concern is clear," then say it again, calmly.
- **Notice the toll.** If you're replaying conversations at night or dreading Mondays, that's your body telling you something. See [Being the only one in the room](#article-anxiety-only-one-in-the-room).
