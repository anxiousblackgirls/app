Healing from racial trauma doesn't mean pretending racism is over or that it didn't hurt. It means building ways to protect yourself, process what happened, and stay connected to your joy and your people.

## What research shows

> Psychologists Riana Elyse Anderson and Howard Stevenson developed the RECAST model, which describes how families can help children cope with racial stress through racial socialization: honest conversations about race, pride in being Black, and practicing how to respond to racism. Their work shows these skills can lower stress and build confidence. Scholars in the American Psychologist special issue on racial trauma also emphasized that healing includes community connection, cultural pride, and collective action, not just individual therapy.

## Ways to heal

- **Pride.** Learning Black history, art, and culture reminds you where your strength comes from.
- **Community.** Spaces with other Black women where you don't have to explain.
- **Processing.** Telling your story to someone who believes you.
- **Body care.** Rest, movement, and practices that calm your nervous system.
- **Action.** Advocacy, voting, mentoring, or organizing, in whatever way fits your energy.
- **Therapy.** Especially with a therapist trained in racial trauma.

## For mothers and caregivers

Talking with your children about race can feel heavy. Research shows it helps them, especially when you balance honesty about racism with pride and practical skills.

## Try this

- **Make a pride list.** Write five things you love about being a Black woman. Read it when the world feels heavy.
- **Practice a response.** Think of a situation you might face, and rehearse what you'd say or do. Practice builds confidence.
- **Do one thing this month for your community,** however small.
- **For parents:** ask your child, "Has anything happened at school that felt unfair because you're Black?" Then listen without rushing to fix it.
