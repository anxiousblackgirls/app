Some losses don't come with a funeral, a casket, or casseroles. They hurt just as much, but people may not see them as losses at all. Grief expert Kenneth Doka called this disenfranchised grief: grief that isn't openly recognized or supported.

## Losses that often go unnamed

- Miscarriage, stillbirth, or infertility
- A parent who is alive but not present, through addiction, estrangement, or dementia
- A loved one who is incarcerated
- A friendship that ended
- A divorce or breakup
- A job, a home, or a community you had to leave
- The life you expected to have

> Pregnancy loss is one example that hits Black women especially hard. National data show that Black women experience stillbirth at more than twice the rate of white women. Yet many women say they were expected to go back to work and act fine within days.

## Why naming it matters

When grief isn't recognized, people often grieve alone. They may feel ashamed of how sad they are, or confused about why they can't "get over it." Naming a loss gives you permission to mourn it.

## Through a Black lens

Our communities are strong at showing up for deaths. We're often less practiced at showing up for losses that come with stigma, like incarceration, addiction, or miscarriage. Many of us keep these griefs private to protect the family.

## Try this

- **Name your unnamed loss.** Finish the sentence: "I'm grieving ___." Say it out loud or write it down.
- **Create a goodbye.** Plant something, write a letter, or hold a small private ceremony for a loss nobody marked.
- **Find others who understand.** There are support groups for pregnancy loss, families of incarcerated people, and adult children of estranged parents. Shared experience lowers shame.
- **Tell one person** what you've lost and what you need from them.
