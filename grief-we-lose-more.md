Many Black women have been to more funerals by 30 than some people attend in a lifetime. That isn't your imagination. Loss comes earlier and more often in Black families, and it shapes how we live, love, and worry.

## What research shows

> A national study led by sociologist Debra Umberson found that Black Americans born in the 1980s were about three times more likely than white Americans to lose their mother by young adulthood, and more than twice as likely to lose their father. Black Americans were twice as likely to lose two or more family members by age 30, and 90 percent more likely to lose four or more by age 65. The researchers described these early losses as a hidden source of racial disadvantage that affects health across a lifetime.

## How repeated loss affects you

- Bracing for bad news whenever the phone rings late
- Feeling numb at funerals, then falling apart weeks later
- Fear of losing the people you have left
- Taking on caregiving or parenting roles for relatives
- Physical health effects: research links early loss to higher stress and health problems later

Repeated loss can also feed anxiety. When you've lost people suddenly, your mind learns to scan for danger.

## There's no timeline

Grief comes in waves. Birthdays, holidays, the anniversary of a death, and even a song in the grocery store can bring it back. That's normal. It doesn't mean you're stuck.

## Try this

- **Make a loss list.** Write the names of the people you've lost and the year. Seeing it all together can help you understand why you feel so tired or on edge.
- **Mark the hard dates** on your calendar in advance, and plan something gentle for those days.
- **Talk about them.** Say their names. Tell a story about them to someone who knew them.
- **Get grief support.** Many hospices offer free grief groups to anyone in the community, not just families they served.
