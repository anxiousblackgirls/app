Some grief is personal. Some is shared by a whole community. Black women have carried both: the loss of our own loved ones, and the weight of watching our people die in the news, in hospitals, and in a pandemic.

## Collective grief

> Researchers estimated that for every American who died of COVID-19, about nine close relatives were left grieving. Because Black Americans died at higher rates during the pandemic, Black families carried an outsized share of that grief. Another national study found that police killings of unarmed Black Americans were followed by more days of poor mental health among Black adults in the same state, even among people who didn't know the person who died.

You can grieve someone you never met. When it's someone who looks like your father, your son, or you, your body takes it personally.

## How it can show up

- Feeling heavy or numb for days after a news event
- Trouble concentrating at work while coworkers carry on as normal
- Anger with nowhere to go
- Fear for your children, partner, or yourself
- Survivor's guilt, or feeling you should be doing more

## Holding collective grief

Collective grief is easier to carry together. Our ancestors survived through community, song, worship, and action. Those tools still work.

## Try this

- **Name what you're feeling** to someone who shares it. "I can't stop thinking about it" is enough.
- **Take a news break.** Read updates once a day and avoid watching videos of violence.
- **Gather.** Attend a vigil, a service, a protest, or a community meal. Being with others turns isolation into shared strength.
- **Act if you can,** in a way that fits your energy: give, vote, volunteer, call a representative, or support a family directly.
- **Rest without guilt.** Caring for yourself keeps you able to show up for your community over time.
