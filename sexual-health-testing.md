Getting tested for STIs can feel loaded. Maybe you worry about what the nurse will think, or what it means about you or your partner. Testing is routine health care, like a Pap smear or a blood pressure check. It says you take care of yourself.

## Why this matters for Black women

Black communities carry more than our share of STIs. The reasons include less access to care and the way infections spread through small, connected sexual networks. Morals have nothing to do with it. In 2022, Black Americans were about 13 percent of the U.S. population but accounted for 31 percent of reported cases of chlamydia, gonorrhea and syphilis. HIV shows the same pattern. In 2021, Black women were 13 percent of women in the U.S. but 54 percent of new HIV diagnoses among women.

PrEP, a daily pill or shot that prevents HIV, works very well, yet few Black people who could benefit are using it. CDC data from 2021 found that 11 percent of Black people who could benefit from PrEP were taking it, compared with 78 percent of white people.

## What testing looks like

The CDC recommends:

- **Everyone ages 13 to 64:** an HIV test at least once, and more often if you have new partners.
- **Sexually active women under 25:** chlamydia and gonorrhea testing every year.
- **Women 25 and older:** chlamydia and gonorrhea testing every year if you have a new partner, more than one partner, or a partner with an STI.
- **During pregnancy:** testing for syphilis, HIV and hepatitis B and C early on.

Most tests are a urine sample, a swab you can often do yourself, or a quick blood draw. Many STIs have no symptoms at all, which is why testing matters even when you feel fine.

## Not every itch is an STI

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is an imbalance of vaginal bacteria, and it isn't an STI. It's common: a national study found it in about half of Black women ages 14 to 49. It can cause discharge or a fishy smell, and it's treatable. If you keep getting BV or yeast infections, ask your provider about it. You don't need to sit with it in silence or scrub it away with douches, which can make it worse.

> Shame keeps a lot of us out of clinics: the fear of being judged, of running into somebody from church, or of what a positive result would mean. Testing protects you and the people you love. If a result comes back positive, most STIs are curable, and HIV is now a manageable condition with treatment.

## Try this

- **Say it plainly at your next visit:** "I'd like to be tested for STIs, including HIV." Tests aren't always automatic.
- **Ask about PrEP** if you have a partner with HIV, partners whose status you don't know, or you don't always use condoms.
- **Make it a ritual.** Get tested with a new partner before you stop using condoms. Make it a date: test, then tacos.
- **Use free and low-cost options.** Local health departments and Planned Parenthood offer testing, often confidentially.
