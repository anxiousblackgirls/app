Becoming a mother changes everything: your body, your schedule, your relationships, and your sense of who you are. For Black women, it can also come with fear that other mothers don't carry in the same way.

## The fear is grounded

> CDC data for 2021 showed Black women died from pregnancy-related causes at about 2.6 times the rate of white women, and most of those deaths were considered preventable. Many Black mothers enter pregnancy knowing these numbers. Research on doula care found that Medicaid patients with doula support had lower cesarean rates and fewer preterm births, which is one reason many Black mothers now seek doulas.

## The identity shift

Researchers sometimes call the transition to motherhood "matrescence," a change as big as adolescence. It's normal to feel:

- Joy and grief at the same time
- Lost about who you are outside of being "Mommy"
- Pressure to do it all perfectly
- Lonely, even with a baby in your arms all day
- Resentful about how much changed for you compared with your partner

## Through a Black lens

Many of us were raised by mothers and grandmothers who did it all without complaining, so asking for help can feel like failing. Black mothers also carry the work of preparing children for a world that may treat them unfairly, starting much earlier than many parents.

## Try this

- **Build your village before the baby comes.** Write down who can bring meals, hold the baby, run errands, or check on you, and ask them now.
- **Consider a doula.** Some states cover doula care through Medicaid. Search for Black doulas in your area.
- **Protect one piece of yourself.** Choose one thing that's yours, like a weekly walk alone or a show you love, and guard it.
- **Watch for postpartum depression and anxiety.** If sadness, worry, or numbness lasts more than two weeks, call the National Maternal Mental Health Hotline at 1-833-852-6262.
