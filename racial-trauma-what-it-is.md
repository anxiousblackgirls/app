Racial trauma, also called race-based traumatic stress, is the emotional and physical harm caused by experiences of racism. It can come from one frightening event, like being harassed or threatened, or from years of smaller insults that add up.

## What research shows

> Psychologist Robert Carter argued that racism can cause real psychological injury, with symptoms like fear, hypervigilance, avoidance, anger, and shame, similar to other forms of trauma. A 2019 special issue of American Psychologist on racial trauma brought together research showing that racism, whether direct, vicarious, or built into systems, affects mental and physical health, and that healing requires attention to both the individual and the community.

## What it can look like

- Feeling on guard in stores, at work, or around police
- Replaying a racist encounter over and over
- Avoiding places or people connected to it
- Anger, sadness, or numbness after news about racist violence
- Headaches, stomach problems, or trouble sleeping
- Feeling you have to be perfect to be safe

## It isn't "being too sensitive"

Many Black women are told they're overreacting or "making everything about race." Your reactions make sense. Your body is responding to real threats, past and present.

## Racial trauma can be passed down

Families who lived through segregation, violence, or migration often carry lessons shaped by those experiences, like strict rules about how to act in public. Some of those lessons protect us. Some keep us afraid.

## Try this

- **Name what happened.** Write down a racist experience that still bothers you. Next to it, write how it affected you then and how it affects you now.
- **Check your body.** When you think about it, where do you feel it? Breathe slowly into that spot for one minute.
- **Talk with someone who gets it.** A friend, family member, or therapist who understands racism can help you feel less alone.
- **Ask a therapist directly:** "Do you have experience treating racial trauma?"
