For a lot of us, sex education was a look across the room, a "keep your legs closed," or a talk that started and ended with getting pregnant. If you grew up in a house or a church where sex was something you didn't discuss, it makes sense that talking about it now feels awkward, or even unsafe.

## Where the silence comes from

Historian Darlene Clark Hine described a "culture of dissemblance": Black women kept their inner lives private, especially anything about sex, as a way to protect themselves from people who already saw them as sexual objects. That protection was real. It also got passed down as silence, and silence leaves daughters guessing.

Research with Black women shows the cost. In one study, Black immigrant women living in the U.S. described mothers who repeated some version of "sex is bad" and used fear to keep them away from it. As adults, many felt their limited knowledge had hurt them. Newer research shows things are shifting. In a 2025 study of 29 Black mothers raising young daughters, many were having direct, open conversations, using real-life examples and talking about the daughter's own feelings and body. Some were teaching their girls to question the messages the world sends about Black women's bodies.

## What silence can cost you as an adult

When nobody gave you words for sex, you may find it hard to ask a partner to use a condom, tell a doctor about pain, say what feels good or say no. You might feel shame about wanting sex at all, or feel like you're doing something wrong when you're curious. None of that is a character flaw. You learned the rules of the house you grew up in.

> Our mothers and grandmothers often stayed quiet about sex to keep us safe in a world that treated Black women's bodies as public property. You can honor why they did it and still choose something different. Speaking up about your body, your health and your pleasure is a way to keep yourself safe too.

## Try this

- **Name one message you got growing up.** Write it down: "Good girls don't..." Then ask, "Do I still believe this?"
- **Start with your doctor.** Practice one sentence: "I have a question about my sexual health." Providers hear these questions every day.
- **Use real words.** Saying "vulva," "vagina" or "discharge" out loud, even alone, makes it easier when it counts.
- **Find one safe person.** A friend, sister or therapist you can ask the questions nobody answered.
- **If you have daughters or nieces,** answer their questions plainly and at their age level. You don't need a perfect speech, just an open door.
