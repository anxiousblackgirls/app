Caring for a parent, a grandparent, a sibling's kids, or a sick partner is an act of love. It's also work, and for many Black women it comes on top of a full-time job and their own families.

## Black women carry much of the caregiving

> National caregiving research from AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving has found that Black caregivers often provide many hours of care a week and are more likely than white caregivers to report financial strain from caregiving. Pew Research Center has described adults in midlife who support both aging parents and children as the "sandwich generation," and many Black women live right in the middle of it.

## Signs of caregiver burnout

- Feeling resentful, then guilty for feeling resentful
- Getting sick more often
- Skipping your own doctor appointments
- Pulling away from friends
- Snapping at the person you care for
- Feeling like there's no "you" left outside of caregiving

## Through a Black lens

In many of our families, caregiving falls to the daughter, and often to the one who "has it together." Asking siblings to share the load can feel like breaking a family rule. Putting a parent in a care facility can feel like abandonment, even when it's the safest choice. You're allowed to need help, and you're allowed to set limits.

## Try this

- **Make the invisible visible.** Write down every task you do for your loved one in a week, with the time each takes. Share the list at a family meeting and ask each person to take one item.
- **Schedule respite.** Ask about adult day programs, home health aides, or a relative who can cover one afternoon a week.
- **Call your local Area Agency on Aging.** Find yours at eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116. They connect caregivers with free and low-cost help.
- **Keep one appointment for yourself** every month, whether it's a doctor, therapist, or hair appointment.
