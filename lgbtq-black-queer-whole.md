Being a Black woman who is lesbian, bisexual, queer, or transgender means living at the meeting point of several identities at once. You don't get to be one of them at a time, and you shouldn't have to choose.

## Intersectionality

Legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw coined the term intersectionality in 1989 to describe how race and gender combine to shape Black women's experiences in ways that neither racism nor sexism alone explains. For Black LGBTQ+ women, sexuality and gender identity add more layers.

## Minority stress

> Psychologist Ilan Meyer's minority stress model explains that LGBTQ+ people face extra stress from prejudice, expecting rejection, hiding their identity, and absorbing negative messages about themselves. That stress helps explain higher rates of anxiety and depression. Researchers studying Black lesbians described "triple jeopardy," the combined stress of racism, sexism, and heterosexism, and also found strong resilience, including pride, community, and spirituality.

## How it can show up

- Feeling not Black enough in LGBTQ+ spaces, and not accepted in some Black spaces
- Hiding parts of yourself at work, church, or family gatherings
- Constant scanning for whether a place is safe
- Anxiety about being outed
- Loneliness, even in a crowd

## Your whole self is welcome here

Your identities aren't problems to manage. They're part of a long line of Black queer and trans women who shaped our culture, including Audre Lorde, Marsha P. Johnson, and Lorraine Hansberry.

## Try this

- **Write your identity map.** In a circle, write all the identities that make you who you are. Notice which ones you feel free to show and where.
- **Find one affirming space,** online or in person, where all of you is welcome.
- **Read or listen to Black queer women's stories.** Audre Lorde's "Sister Outsider" is a good place to start.
- **Look for an affirming therapist.** The National Queer and Trans Therapists of Color Network (nqttcn.com) lists therapists who understand.
